# The Story Behind Insect Hotels

**Where they came from, where they are, and why you should build one.**

![A handcrafted insect hotel: shelter, solitude, and a small act of restoration](images/20260228_124038.jpg){ width="100%" }

You're reading this because you're a human. We established that already. But
the fact that you're still here, curious about a hotel you can never check into,
says something rather good about you.

So here's the story.

## A Brief History of the Insect Hotel

The idea of providing artificial nesting sites for insects is older than you
might think, but the modern **insect hotel** as we know it emerged in
**Europe in the late 1980s and 1990s**, primarily in **Germany and the
Netherlands**.

The problem was straightforward. Intensive forestry had tidied Europe's forests
to within an inch of their lives. Dead wood was cleared. Log piles were removed.
Hollow stems were mowed. The untidy corners where solitary bees, wasps, and
beetles had nested for millennia were simply gone.

Forest managers, recognising somewhat belatedly that the insects they'd
displaced were the same ones pollinating crops and controlling pests, began
providing drilled wooden blocks and bundles of hollow twigs as replacement
nesting sites. These early structures were functional, not decorative. They were
apologies in timber.

By the **2000s**, insect hotels had spread across Europe and evolved from
forestry tools into garden features, school projects, and public art
installations. Conservation organisations like **Buglife** in the UK and the
**Scottish Wildlife Trust** championed them. The concept crossed the Atlantic,
appeared in Australia and South Africa, and became one of the most accessible
entry points into practical conservation.

Today, they are everywhere: in back gardens, on balconies, in schoolyards,
at wine estates, and in the courtyards of architecture museums. The humble
insect hotel has become a global movement.

## Notable Insect Hotels Around the World

Not all insect hotels are modest garden installations. Some have become
landmarks in their own right.

### :material-trophy: The World Record Holder, Highland Titles, Scotland

In March 2022, conservation company **Highland Titles** set a **Guinness World
Record** for the largest insect hotel ever built: a colossal **199.9 cubic
metre** structure on their nature reserve at Duror in the Scottish Highlands.
Built from felled Sitka spruce, bamboo canes, masonry bricks, forest bark, and
wood chippings, it took six months and seven staff members to complete. It
broke the previous record of 89.37 m³, held by the Polish Association of
Developers in Warsaw.

The structure is expected to eventually house millions of insects (ants,
ladybirds, beetles, bees, and butterflies) all in the shadow of the Scottish
mountains.

### :material-flower: Boschendal Wine Estate, Franschhoek, South Africa

![The insect hotel at Boschendal Wine Estate: logs, bamboo, bark, and pine cones packed into a sprawling structure](images/Boschendal_Insect_Hotel.jpg){ width="100%" }

**Boschendal** is one of the oldest wine estates in South Africa, with title
deeds dating to **1685**. Nestled between Franschhoek and Stellenbosch in the
Western Cape, deep inside the Cape Floral Kingdom, it is home to over **600
species of fynbos** across **1,000 hectares of protected nature reserve**.

The estate's insect hotel sits within **The Tree House**, a dedicated
children's activity area tucked under beautiful plane trees near the main
parking area. Shortlisted for the **We Are Africa 2019 Design Award**, The Tree
House combines the insect hotel with seed propagation stations, farm foraging,
nature walks, and activities that teach children about climate change and
biodiversity.

But the insect hotel is more than a children's attraction. It's part of
Boschendal's deep commitment to **regenerative agriculture**. The estate has
planted **600,000 new fruit trees**, is certified as one of only **38 WWF
Conservation Champions** in South Africa, and works with grass-fed cattle,
forest-fed pigs, and free-range chickens to restore soil ecology. In a
landscape where fynbos, fruit orchards, and mountain slopes intertwine, the
insect hotel is a small but deliberate piece of a larger ecological puzzle.

### :material-home-city: Alusta Pavilion, Helsinki, Finland

In 2022, architects **Maiju Suomi** and **Elina Koivisto** from **Aalto
University** created **Alusta**, a giant, human-friendly insect hotel in the
courtyard between Helsinki's Museum of Finnish Architecture and the Design
Museum. Built with the help of 50 architecture students, the pavilion features
clay walls that double as seating, grow bags containing over a thousand plants,
and structures designed to shelter and feed urban pollinators.

*"Many visitors have exclaimed that this is like a giant insect hotel,"* said
Suomi. That was entirely the point. Alusta is both a gathering place for humans
and a habitat for insects, a deliberate blurring of the line between
architecture and ecology.

### :material-hexagon-multiple: The Arup Insect Hotel, City of London

In 2010, the International Year of Biodiversity, engineering firm **Arup
Associates** won the **"Beyond the Hive"** competition to design an insect
habitat for **St Dunstan in the East**, a ruined church garden in the City of
London. Their creation is a bio-mimetic sculpture constructed from **25 layers
of birch plywood**, CNC-cut into a **Voronoi pattern**, the organic,
irregular geometry found in dragonfly wings and soap bubbles. Different
compartments house rotting logs for stag beetles, vertical slots for
butterflies, and hollow tubes for solitary bees. It's engineering and ecology,
speaking the same language.

### :material-flower-tulip: The Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, London

**Kew Gardens** maintains bug hotels throughout its grounds, including a
purpose-built **Insect and Bee Tower** in the Natural Area. Kew has also
partnered with **Taylors of Harrogate** to create what they called *"the most
amazing luxury bee hotel ever"*, a project designed to promote pollinator
conservation and raise awareness among the garden's millions of annual visitors.

### :material-palette: Vallmora Park, Barcelona, Spain

Architecture firm **Batlleiroig** designed a permanent insect hotel as part of
the **Vallmora Park** project in El Masnou, near Barcelona. Nominated for the
**Landezine International Landscape Award**, it serves as both ecological
infrastructure and a pedagogical tool, a structure where children and adults
can observe insect life cycles in a designed landscape.

### :material-book-open-variant: And Countless Others

Insect hotels have appeared at the **Krakow Zoo** in Poland, in the gardens of
**Sonoma State University** in California, in community gardens across France
(where over 900 towns are now pesticide-free), and in thousands of private
gardens, school grounds, and nature reserves worldwide. A youth club in Germany
once combined **40 individual insect hotels** into a single striking high-rise.
The format is endlessly adaptable, and that's part of its power.

## The Network: Pollinator Pathways and Bee Highways

Insects don't read maps. But they do follow corridors of habitat, and across
Europe and beyond, people are building them.

### :material-bee: B-Lines, United Kingdom

**Buglife**, the UK invertebrate conservation charity, launched the
**B-Lines** initiative, a strategically mapped network of **3-kilometre-wide
insect pathways** running through every county in the UK, connecting existing
wildlife areas together. The context is stark: **over 97% of England's
flower-rich grasslands have been lost since the 1930s**, an area the size of
Wales. B-Lines aims to restore at least **150,000 hectares** of flower-rich
habitat, with insect hotels serving as stepping stones along the routes.

### :material-highway: The Pollinator Passage, Oslo, Norway

In 2015, **ByBi** ("CityBee"), an urban beekeeping organisation in Oslo,
launched the **Pollinator Passage**, a citizen-driven project to create
feeding stations, gardens, and shelters arranged across the city and linked to
form a **bee highway**. Their website hosts an interactive map where residents
can register their own sites, identify gaps in the network, and see where more
habitat is needed. It's grassroots ecology at its finest: everyone does
something small, and the network grows.

### :material-leaf: Dublin's Lifeline, Ireland

**Bi Urban**, a social enterprise in Dublin's Stoneybatter neighbourhood, is
working to create a **"Lifeline"**, an ecological corridor connecting the
Royal Canal to the River Liffey, providing habitat for both residents and
local wildlife along the route.

### :material-road-variant: The Pollinator Pathway, Seattle, USA

In 2007, artist and designer **Sarah Bergmann** created the **Pollinator
Pathway**, a **mile-long, twelve-foot-wide corridor** of pollinator-friendly
gardens along Seattle's Columbia Street, connecting Seattle University's campus
to Nora's Woods pocket park. What began as a single urban art-and-ecology
project has since spread to over **300 towns across 24 US states** and parts of
Canada. Bergmann describes it as *"a participatory art, design and ecology
social sculpture initiative"*, proof that a good idea, planted in the right
soil, can grow far beyond its origins.

### :material-connection: The Bigger Picture

These initiatives represent a growing understanding: **a single insect hotel
helps, but a connected network of habitats transforms**. Pollinator pathways
link gardens, parks, verges, window boxes, and rooftop plantings into
continuous corridors that allow bees, butterflies, and hoverflies to move
safely through landscapes that would otherwise be hostile.

Even the smallest contribution (a flowering window box, a patch of unmowed
lawn, a bamboo-tube bee house on a balcony railing) becomes part of something
larger when it connects to its neighbours.

## Why You Should Build One

You can't check into The Insect Hotel. But you can build your own. Here's why
you should.

### :material-sprout: Because the habitat is disappearing

Globally, insect biomass is declining by an estimated **1 to 2.5 percent per
year**. Habitat loss, pesticide use, light pollution, and climate change are all
contributing. The tidy gardens, paved driveways, and manicured lawns that humans
prefer are ecological deserts for insects. The fallen leaves, dead wood, and
hollow stems that insects need have been swept, cleared, and mowed into
oblivion. An insect hotel, even a small one, provides nesting and
overwintering habitat where none existed before.

### :material-food-apple: Because your food depends on it

Pollinators, including solitary bees, hoverflies, and butterflies, are
responsible for pollinating roughly **75% of the world's food crops**. In Europe
alone, pollination services are estimated to be worth at least **$25 billion**
annually. Every solitary bee that nests in your insect hotel contributes to this
essential service. A single red mason bee can pollinate the equivalent of
**120 honeybees' worth** of apple blossom.

### :material-shield-bug: Because they're your garden's pest control

Insect hotels attract **lacewings**, whose larvae devour aphids by the hundred.
They attract **ladybirds**, each of which can eat up to **5,000 aphids** in its
lifetime. They attract **solitary wasps**, which hunt caterpillars, beetle
larvae, and other garden pests. An insect hotel is not just shelter; it's a
biological pest control system that works while you sleep.

### :material-school: Because they teach

An insect hotel in a school garden, a community space, or your own back yard is
a living lesson in ecology. Children (and adults) learn to observe, to identify
species, to understand seasonal cycles, and to appreciate the extraordinary
complexity of the small creatures that sustain the ecosystems we depend on.

### :material-hammer-wrench: Because anyone can do it

You don't need land. You don't need money. You don't need expertise. A few
bamboo tubes bundled together and hung on a sunny wall is a perfectly functional
bee hotel. A stack of old pallets filled with pine cones, bark, straw, and
drilled logs is a five-star insect condominium. The materials are free. The
construction is forgiving. And the guests are not fussy reviewers.

## Who Will Check In?

Build it, and they will come. Here's who you can expect.

| Guest | What they need | What they give back |
|-------|---------------|-------------------|
| :material-bee: **Solitary bees** (mason bees, leafcutter bees) | Hollow tubes (bamboo, drilled wood), 6–10 mm diameter | Pollination, far more efficient per bee than honeybees |
| :material-ladybug: **Ladybirds** | Pine cones, bark crevices, dry leaves | Aphid control, up to 5,000 aphids per lifetime |
| :material-butterfly: **Lacewings** | Straw, rolled cardboard, bark strips | Larvae eat aphids, mites, and small caterpillars |
| :material-bee: **Solitary wasps** | Hollow tubes, drilled wood, 3–8 mm diameter | Pest control: they hunt caterpillars and beetle larvae |
| :material-butterfly: **Butterflies** | Vertical slits in wood panels | Pollination, beauty, and an indicator of ecosystem health |
| :material-bug: **Beetles** | Loose bark, dead wood, log sections | Decomposition, breaking down dead plant material and enriching soil |
| :material-bug: **Earwigs** | Upturned pots filled with straw or hay | Pest control: they eat aphids, mites, and insect eggs |

## A Few Honest Warnings

Because we believe in transparency (see: [House Rules](house-rules.md)).

!!! note "Bigger is not always better"
    Research has shown that very large, densely packed insect hotels can
    increase the risk of **disease and parasitism** among residents. In nature,
    solitary bees nest in small, widely spaced groups. A modest hotel with
    well-chosen materials, cleaned and maintained annually, is more effective
    than an enormous structure left to deteriorate.

!!! note "Location matters"
    Face your insect hotel **south to south-east** (in the Southern Hemisphere,
    north to north-east). Place it at least a metre off the ground, sheltered
    from prevailing wind and rain. Position it near **flowering plants**,
    especially native species with strong UV nectar guides that your future
    guests can actually see (even if [you cannot](not-an-insect.md)).

!!! note "Not all hotels attract the right guests"
    A 2020 study in Marseille found that urban bee hotels were primarily used by
    an **exotic bee species**, whose presence correlated with lower native bee
    numbers in the area. Pair your insect hotel with **native plantings** to
    support local species, not invasive ones.

## Start Small. Start Now.

You don't need to break a world record. You don't need a thousand hectares of
fynbos. You need a drill, a block of untreated hardwood, and ten minutes.

Drill holes between 6 and 10 mm in diameter, at least 10 cm deep, angled
slightly upward so rain drains out. Sand the entrances smooth. Fix the block to
a sunny, sheltered wall. Wait.

Within weeks, sometimes days, a solitary bee will find it. She'll inspect the
holes, choose one, provision it with pollen, lay an egg, and seal the entrance
with mud or leaf fragments. By next spring, a new generation will emerge.

You'll have built a hotel. A tiny one. But a real one.

And somewhere, on a wine estate in Franschhoek, on a nature reserve in the
Scottish Highlands, in a courtyard in Helsinki, and in thousands of gardens in
between, the network grows.
